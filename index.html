🧵 Synthesis

Thesis: whether you solve a problem, use an analogy or produce a creative idea depends on one move: getting past the first representation your mind offers. A problem is a gap between an initial and a goal state; well-structured gaps can be crossed with algorithms or heuristics such as means-ends analysis (the strategy of Newell & Simon’s GPS) and hill climbing, while ill-structured problems are mainly representation problems (Matlin; Sternberg). Analogies are the most powerful heuristic, but people mostly notice surface features, so the bottleneck is noticing: comparing two analogues, enacting, listening and asking ‘why’ help (Gick & Holyoak; Catrambone; Markman; Chi). Mental sets, functional fixedness, stereotypes, negative transfer and transparency keep us inside an old frame; insight is the moment the frame changes, which feels sudden (Metcalfe), has a neural signature and can be helped by incubation and sleep. Camarda et al. (2018) show the same logic in creativity: System 1 delivers dominant ideas, and inhibitory control (not working memory) is needed to push them aside so System 2 can expand. Still debated: whether disinhibition can help, whether insight is special or gradual, and how well lab analogies reflect real life.

Readings: Matlin, Cognition (pp. 380–387) · Robinson-Riegler, Cognitive Psychology (pp. 512–520) · Sternberg, Sternberg & Mio, Cognitive Psychology (pp. 454–465) · Camarda et al. (2018), Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts, 12(3), 351–358.

🧭 Understanding the problem

Initial state, goal state, obstacles, and why the representation decides everything

  • A problem has three parts: the initial state (where you are), the goal state (where you want to be) and the obstacles in between.
  • To understand a problem you need an accurate mental representation: symbols, lists, matrices, hierarchical trees, diagrams or visual images.
  • Attention matters: it is limited, competing thoughts divide it, and you must aim it at the relevant part of the problem.
  • Situated cognition: cues in the environment shape how you represent a problem.
  • Embodied cognition: your body helps: arm-swinging ‘exercise breaks’ led more people to solve the two-rope problem; moving your hands improves mental rotation; earlier gestures help with a mechanical problem.
🪝 Memory hook: Start → obstacles → goal: you can’t cross a river you haven’t drawn on the map.

⚖️ Algorithms vs heuristics

Guaranteed but slow, or fast but risky: the anagram test

  • An algorithm always produces a solution, but can be very inefficient. Example: an exhaustive search that tries every possible answer.
  • SIMULTANEOUSLY has 14 letters: more than 87 billion arrangements to try one by one.
  • A heuristic is a general rule that is usually correct: you ignore some options and explore only the most promising ones (Nickerson).
  • Heuristics carry no guarantee: the rule ‘reject rare starts like LY’ makes you miss LYMPHOID (from IPMHYLOD).
  • Psychologists study heuristics more, because people solve most everyday problems with them. The three classics: analogy, means-ends, hill climbing.
🪝 Memory hook: Algorithm = the slow, sure turtle 🐢. Heuristic = the fast hare 🐇 who sometimes takes the wrong path.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Heuristics trade certainty for speed: weigh how fast they are against the risk of missing the answer.
Tap to open ▸

🐦 The analogy approach

Using an old solution for a new problem, and problem isomorphs

  • The analogy approach: you use the solution to a similar, earlier problem to solve a new one (statistics examples, an old essay strategy).
  • Analogies are everywhere: engineers made 102 analogies in 9 hours of problem solving (Christensen & Schunn, 2007).
  • Students in Brazil, India and the US preferred the analogy approach as their strategy (Güss & Wiley, 2007).
  • Analogies drive creative breakthroughs: the Wright brothers copied how birds adjust their wing tips to steer.
  • Problem isomorphs = problems with the same underlying structure and solution but different details. The challenge is to peel away the superficial details.
🪝 Memory hook: 🐦→✈️ The Wright idea: a bird’s wing tip taught a plane to turn.
★ KEY INSIGHT · The hard part of an analogy is seeing the same skeleton under different clothes.

If you once fixed a wobbly table with folded paper, and now your laptop stand wobbles, you reach for paper again: that is the analogy approach.

📦 Surface vs structure: why analogies fail

The Rutgers–FedEx case and what helps people transfer

  • People focus on surface features (the specific objects and words) instead of structural features (the underlying core).
  • Rutgers first copied other universities (surface match: not effective), then saw the real problem was tracking and copied FedEx parcel tracking: a great solution.
  • People often fail to transfer: the same problem in a new setting or with a new ‘cover story’ goes unsolved (situated cognition).
  • People with limited problem-solving skills and metacognition struggle most with analogies.
  • What helps: first try several structurally similar problems (Bassok, 2003); practise sorting problems by structure (Quilici & Mayer, 2002).
🪝 Memory hook: Don’t judge a problem by its cover story.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Ask what a problem is really about (its structure), not what it looks like.
Tap to open ▸
Rutgers asked ‘who looks like us?’ and got nowhere. Then it asked ‘who else has to track things?’ and found FedEx. Same lesson for you: ask what the problem DOES, not what it looks like.

🎯 The means-ends heuristic

Sub-problems, closing the gap, and the courage to step backward

  • Two parts: (1) divide the problem into sub-problems; (2) reduce the difference between the current state and the goal for each one.
  • ‘Ends’ = the result you want; ‘means’ = the methods to get there. One of the most effective, flexible strategies (Dunbar; Lovett).
  • The stapler story: hem loose, talk in 10 minutes → sub-problem 1: find an object that can fix a hem; sub-problem 2: locate it.
  • Greeno (1974): in Elves-and-Goblins people pause when they start a new sub-problem; working memory is busy planning those move sequences.
  • Sometimes you must move backward (step 6: two creatures go back) and people resist this detour.
🪝 Memory hook: Means-ends = Mind the gap: measure it, split it, close it piece by piece.
★ KEY INSIGHT · The best way forward is sometimes a temporary step backward.
Tap to open ▸
Big goal, too far away? Break it into stepping stones. Sometimes a stepping stone is behind you, and you still have to step back on it.

💻 Computer simulation: the General Problem Solver

How Newell & Simon taught a computer to think like you

  • Computer simulation: write a program that performs a task the way a human does, false starts included: no better, no worse.
  • Test it by comparing its steps with human steps on the same problem.
  • General Problem Solver (GPS), Newell & Simon (1972): basic strategy = means-ends analysis, including difference reduction; built from people’s think-aloud reports.
  • GPS was the first program to simulate many human symbolic behaviours, but it was discarded: real-life problems are not clear-cut enough.
  • Anderson’s ACT-R simulations (algebra, geometry, computing) became ‘cognitive tutors’ for high-school maths.
🪝 Memory hook: GPS = the Great Pretend Student: a program that copies how we reason, mistakes included.
★ KEY INSIGHT · A simulation is a theory you can run: if it behaves like people, the theory is plausible.
Tap to open ▸
Psychologists recorded people thinking aloud, then wrote a program that follows the same steps. If the program stumbles where people stumble, the theory of how people think is probably right.

⛰️ The hill-climbing heuristic

Always take the steepest step… and risk a dead end

  • At every choice point, choose the option that seems to lead most directly to the goal (like taking the steepest path up a hill).
  • Useful when you can only see the next step and lack information about the alternatives.
  • Biggest drawback: you may skip an indirect route with bigger long-term benefits; the steep path can end abruptly.
  • Example: taking a job right after graduation for a high salary, when a graduate degree might pay more in the long run.
  • It favours short-term goals over long-term solutions; no guarantee of reaching the top.
🪝 Memory hook: Hill climbing = always uphill, never a step down → stuck on the wrong peak 🚩.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Hill climbing encourages short-term goals rather than long-term solutions.
Tap to open ▸
It’s like always choosing the queue that looks shortest right now: often fine, sometimes it’s the one that stops moving.

🌍 Cross-national differences in strategies

Güss, Tuason & Gerhard (2010): microworlds and planning

  • Many problems are too complex for classic strategies. Güss et al. studied ≈100 students in each of 5 countries: US, Philippines, Germany, Brazil, India.
  • Think-aloud method: report your thoughts while working, without interpreting them, in a language you are fluent in.
  • Microworlds: computer simulations of complex real-life problems, e.g. commanding a fire brigade; 300+ options, the situation changes even if you do nothing, more than one right answer, ≈90 min.
  • Hypothesis: resource-rich countries plan more. Result: Germans made the most planning comments, Americans, Brazilians and Indians intermediate, Filipinos the fewest.
  • Limit: groups differ on many dimensions, so research can find a cultural difference but not clearly explain it.
🪝 Memory hook: 🇩🇪 plans most, 🇵🇭 least, and ‘country’ is never the only difference.

♟️ Means-end analysis and the mutilated checkerboard

Transformation problems, and an analogy almost nobody notices

  • Means-end analysis (GPS): break a problem into sub-goals; each sub-goal brings you closer. Ideal for transformation problems (start → series of transformations → goal), e.g. writing papers and studying for several exams.
  • Mutilated checkerboard: cut off two opposite corners; can 31 dominoes cover the 62 squares?
  • Answer: impossible. Each domino covers one dark and one light square, and both removed squares have the same colour.
  • It is the same structure as the country-dance problem (unequal men and women can’t all be paired).
  • Analogies are potentially the most powerful heuristic, but people rarely use them unless they are ‘practically hit over the head’ with the connection.

☢️ Duncker’s radiation problem and Gick & Holyoak (1980)

Does reading a similar story help you solve a hard problem?

  • Radiation problem (Duncker): a tumour can’t be operated on; strong rays destroy healthy tissue, weak rays don’t affect the tumour.
  • Solution = convergence: aim many weak rays from different angles so they add up only at the tumour.
  • Source story: a commander sends a few tanks over each of several bridges to an island HQ. Given as a ‘story-comprehension’ task, then the target radiation problem.
  • Target only: 10% found the convergence solution; source memorised first: 30%.
  • So only about 20% spontaneously noticed and used the analogy.
🪝 Memory hook: 10 → 30 → only 20 noticed: the source story was there, but most people never looked.

📊 Gick & Holyoak (1983): what makes transfer happen?

Principle, diagram, or a second analogue?

  • Three hints were added to one analogous story: a general principle, a diagram of converging arrows, or another analogous problem.
  • General principle: 29% vs 32% → no help. Diagram: 40% vs 22% → no help.
  • Two analogues that participants had to relate to each other: 21% → 52%. Only this worked.
  • Comparing the two stories forces mapping, which produces schema induction: a mental representation of the shared principle.
  • This is transfer-appropriate processing: finding a relationship in phase 1 is exactly what phase 2 requires.
🪝 Memory hook: Two stories > one story + a hint: compare, then the schema clicks.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Comparing two analogues builds a schema; a stated rule or a diagram does not.

Being told the rule or seeing a drawing didn’t help. What helped was comparing two different stories that hide the same trick. Comparing makes you find the trick yourself, and then you recognise it next time.

🔎 Noticing, mapping and schema building

Why analogies usually fail at step 1

  • Three processes (Novick & Holyoak, 1991): noticing a relationship, mapping the elements onto each other, building a schema.
  • Mapping example: the tumour = the military headquarters; the rays = the army; healthy tissue = the mined roads or bridges.
  • The schema here = the convergence schema: many weak forces converge on one target.
  • Noticing is the main culprit (Holyoak & Koh, 1987; Ross, 1987): basically a failure of memory; the new problem doesn’t trigger the old one.
  • A hint works as a retrieval cue, but in daily life nobody gives you hints.
🪝 Memory hook: Notice → Map → Schema: N-M-S, ‘Never Miss Similarities’.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Most analogy failures are memory failures: the old problem never comes to mind.

🖨️ Surface vs structural features (Gentner)

The 2 × 2 that predicts when analogies work

  • Surface features = the specific elements of a problem; problems with surface similarity look alike.
  • Structural features = the underlying relationships among those elements.

🧵 Synthesis

Thesis: whether you solve a problem, use an analogy or produce a creative idea depends on one move: getting past the first representation your mind offers. A problem is a gap between an initial and a goal state; well-structured gaps can be crossed with algorithms or heuristics such as means-ends analysis (the strategy of Newell & Simon’s GPS) and hill climbing, while ill-structured problems are mainly representation problems (Matlin; Sternberg). Analogies are the most powerful heuristic, but people mostly notice surface features, so the bottleneck is noticing: comparing two analogues, enacting, listening and asking ‘why’ help (Gick & Holyoak; Catrambone; Markman; Chi). Mental sets, functional fixedness, stereotypes, negative transfer and transparency keep us inside an old frame; insight is the moment the frame changes, which feels sudden (Metcalfe), has a neural signature and can be helped by incubation and sleep. Camarda et al. (2018) show the same logic in creativity: System 1 delivers dominant ideas, and inhibitory control (not working memory) is needed to push them aside so System 2 can expand. Still debated: whether disinhibition can help, whether insight is special or gradual, and how well lab analogies reflect real life.

A3
Which well-known problem-solving strategies are there? (part 2)

Short answer: Means-ends analysis: split the problem into sub-goals and reduce the difference to each (simulated by the General Problem Solver). Hill climbing: always take the step that seems closest to the goal, which can miss better detours.

  • 🎯 Means-ends heuristic: (1) divide the problem into sub-problems, (2) reduce the difference between current state and goal for each. One of the most effective and flexible strategies.
  • 📎 Example: a skirt hem comes loose 10 minutes before a talk → sub-goals ‘find a hem-fixer’ + ‘locate it’ → a stapler (Matlin).
  • ⏸️ People pause before each sub-problem in Elves & Goblins (Greeno, 1974), and resist moves that temporarily increase the distance to the goal (step 6 goes backwards).
  • 💻 General Problem Solver (Newell & Simon, 1972): a computer simulation of means-ends analysis built from think-aloud protocols; later dropped because it was not general enough. Anderson’s ACT-R models led to cognitive tutors.
  • ⛰️ Hill climbing: at every choice point take the step that seems to lead most directly to the goal; useful with little information, but favours short-term goals and can lead to dead ends.

  • Step 1: define initial and goal state. Initial: all 4 disks on rod A. Goal: all 4 on rod C, in the same order.
  • Step 2: find the most important difference. The largest disk is not on C. Everything else depends on this, because it must go to the bottom.
  • Step 3: set a sub-goal to remove that difference. ‘Move the largest disk to C’. That move is blocked by the three smaller disks, which creates a new sub-goal: ‘move the 3-disk tower to B’.
  • Step 4: apply the same analysis to each sub-goal (recursively). To move 3 disks to B, first move 2 disks to C, and so on. Each sub-goal is a smaller version of the same problem.
  • Step 5: execute, check the difference, choose the next sub-goal. Once the largest disk is on C, the new goal is to move the 3-disk tower from B onto C.

Watch out: means-end analysis sometimes requires moves that seem to increase the distance to the goal (putting disks on B, the ‘wrong’ rod). People resist such detours (Matlin; Elves & Goblins). Greeno (1974) showed people pause when they start a new sub-problem. The fewest possible moves is 2⁴ − 1 = 15.

  • The WM task may have been too easy or not demanding enough compared with the incongruent Stroop; it only requires storing dot positions, not manipulating them. The authors admit this and suggest a harder (backward-recall) version.
  • Support for the claim rests on a null result, which cannot prove absence of an effect (sample sizes of ~25 per group).
  • The two tasks differ in more ways than ‘inhibition vs WM’ (visuospatial vs colour-word, response format, timing), and were tested in different samples rather than in one design.
  • In favour: a pilot study (n = 21) showed the high-demand task was harder (82% vs 93% accuracy; slower), and the task has been used successfully in reasoning studies and correlates with executive tasks.

So Experiment 2 makes the inhibition-specific explanation more plausible, but does not fully rule out a general executive account.

In general: System 1 is fast, intuitive and heuristic. In idea generation it automatically produces the most accessible, common ideas (the ‘path of least resistance’), which causes fixation (slow the fall, protect the egg). System 2 is slow and deliberate and enables generative reasoning: conceptual expansion and analogical thinking. To be original you must first inhibit System 1’s dominant ideas, then explore with System 2 (Cassotti et al., 2016a).

Experiment 1: the incongruent Stroop uses up inhibitory control (you must suppress reading the word). With less inhibition available, participants could not push away the dominant System 1 ideas, so they produced fewer ideas (fluidity 7.04 vs 10.69) and fewer ideas outside the fixation categories (expansivity 1.73 vs 3.12).

The congruent Stroop also demanded attention and a second task but no inhibition, and did not reduce creativity (10.43; 2.82). That shows it is the inhibition component, not just doing two things at once, that matters.

So the results support the dual-process model and contradict the disinhibition view (Radel et al., 2015). Note that Robinson-Riegler warns that the System 1/System 2 mapping is not always neat (heuristics can be System 2, practised algorithms System 1).

Thesis: solving a problem, finding an analogy and having a creative idea all depend on the same move: getting past the first representation your mind offers. Well-structured problems can be solved by stepping through a defined problem space with algorithms or heuristics like means-ends analysis and hill climbing. Ill-structured and insight problems fail at the representation stage: mental sets, functional fixedness and surface features keep us inside a familiar frame (Luchins; Gick & Holyoak). Analogies work when we see structure rather than surface, which comparison, enactment and asking ‘why’ encourage; insight is the moment the representation is restructured (Metcalfe & Wiebe). Creativity research makes the mechanism explicit: our intuitive System 1 serves up dominant ideas, and inhibitory control is needed to push them aside so System 2 can expand into original territory (Cassotti et al.; Camarda et al., 2018). Where the science is still debated: whether disinhibition can also help creativity (Radel et al., 2015), whether insight is truly special or gradual, and how well lab analogies reflect real-world problem solving.

✅ Quick check: why do the authors need the congruent Stroop condition?

Because it adds a second task that needs attention but not inhibition. If only the incongruent condition lowers creativity, the cost can be pinned on inhibition, not on doing two things at once.

The Two Experiments and What They Show

❓ This lecture answers: Which prediction came true, was the cost specific to inhibition, and what are the limits?
C.2.1
Experiment 1: loading inhibition cuts creative output

📉 N = 78 (75 analysed). Fluidity F(2, 72) = 3.52, p = .03: inhibition 7.04 vs single 10.69 (d = .72), control 10.43 (d = .05 vs single). Expansivity 1.73 vs 3.12, p = .03, d = .61. Flexibility dropped (r = .82 with fluidity).

C.2.2
Why a second experiment?

❓ Inhibition-specific or a general executive cost? Swap in a working-memory task: the dot memory task (De Neys, 2006), complex vs line patterns. Control study (n = 21): 1,867 vs 1,649 ms; 82% vs 93%.

C.2.3
What the study shows, and what it does not

✅ WM load: no effect (F < 1). Inhibition is a core process; not all executive functions are involved; first dual-task evidence. “Too easy” alternative answered with Miyake et al. (2001); a backward-order version is suggested.

🧭A1

See it, then choose

A problem is a gap from start to goal; represent it first (your body can help). Algorithm: sure, slow. Heuristic: fast, no guarantee. Experience decides whether gut or analysis works (Pretz).
🐦A2

Analogy

Reuse an old solution. People miss it because they see surface, not structure; noticing is a memory failure. Two analogues (52%), acting out (.52) or a hint help; a diagram does not.
🗼A3

Means-ends & hill climbing

Means-ends: subgoals, close each gap, even via a step back (GPS). Hill climbing: most direct next step; quick but can stop on a local peak.
💡B1

Insight

Ill-structured problems need restructuring. Insight stays “cold” then jumps (Metcalfe), but is often prepared and can be gradual; right-hemisphere signature.
🚧B2

Obstacles & aids

Mental set (64–83%), functional fixedness and stereotypes block us. Transfer works if you match relations, not content; beware transparency. Incubation helps after preparation.
🥚C1

The creativity debate

Disinhibition: relax control. Dual-process: stop the obvious System 1 idea, then expand with System 2. Egg task: fluidity, flexibility, expansivity.
📊C2

The evidence

Busy inhibition cut ideas (7.04 vs 10.69, d = .72) and original ideas (d = .61); a busy working memory did not. Creativity needs one specific kind of control.
💬 The whole theme to a friend: “To solve a problem you first have to see it clearly, then pick a strategy: a sure but slow method, or a shortcut like borrowing an old solution, breaking it into steps, or heading for whatever looks like progress. The catch is that we look at what problems look like, not how they work, so we miss useful analogies and fall for false ones. Some problems need a whole new view, and that click feels sudden even though you worked for it. What stops the click is your own experience: the habit, the usual use of an object, the obvious idea. And creativity isn’t switching your brain’s brakes off; it’s using them to stop the obvious idea, so you have room to find a better one.”
